Cambridge AS & A Level IT · Monitoring and Control

Monitoring and Control

Computers can watch the physical world through sensors, and act on it through actuators. This page covers how monitoring systems measure and record, why sensors need calibrating, and how control systems keep greenhouses, heating, car parks and smart homes running by themselves.

Monitoring technologies 3.1

Monitoring systems

  • Monitoring (measuring) uses a computer or microprocessor-based device to measure a physical variable over a period of time.
  • Monitoring systems process and record data so that people can see trends in the changes taking place in the environment being monitored.
  • They use sensors, which convert physical variables such as temperature, light or pressure into a signal that can be measured electrically.
  • The sensors continually send data back to the computer or microprocessor.
  • Most sensors used in monitoring are analogue, so the data sent to the computer is analogue. It is converted into digital form by an analogue to digital converter (ADC) so the computer can process it.
  • The output is usually on a screen or printed out, but can be a warning sound if the monitoring is critical, for example in a nuclear reactor.
  • Monitoring is continuous and never ends.

Sensors used in monitoring

Humidity sensors measure air humidity in weather stations; moisture sensors are also used to monitor soil quality.

Uses of monitoring technologies

Weather stations can measure temperature, rainfall, hours of sunlight, atmospheric pressure, humidity and UV radiation. For this they need:

  • Temperature sensors — to measure the ambient temperature, meaning the temperature of the air surrounding the weather station.
  • Pressure sensors — to measure atmospheric pressure, the pressure of the air above us. Readings are used to make weather forecasts: high pressure indicates sunny weather, low pressure indicates wet and windy weather.
  • Humidity sensors — to measure absolute and relative humidity. Absolute humidity is the amount of moisture in the air, measured without taking temperature into account, as water per cubic metre of air. Relative humidity takes the air temperature into account and is a percentage: the amount of moisture in the air compared with the maximum the air could hold at that temperature. It is used to predict the likelihood of rain and snow.
  • Light sensors — to measure the hours of sunlight. This needs an array of light sensors that together measure the intensity of the light radiation.
  • A tipping bucket and a reed switch — to measure rainfall. The bucket tips when rain has reached a certain weight, empties, then tips back to collect more. Each tip activates a reed switch, which sends a signal to the microprocessor. The microprocessor counts the tips and calculates the rainfall by multiplying the tip count by the amount of rain needed to tip the bucket.

Processing in a weather station

  • Once the computer receives the data from the ADC it stores it in a table, which could be a spreadsheet or database, so it can be processed.
  • Processing might work out, for each day, the highest, lowest and mean temperature, the level of UV radiation, total rainfall, hours of sunshine, the highest and lowest atmospheric pressure, and wind speed and direction.
  • These values can also be calculated for the month and the year to date. Results can be output as graphs, on a monitor or printed.
  • All of this happens automatically, without human intervention.

Monitoring water pollution

  • There are two ways of carrying out water pollution studies.
  • Method 1 — compare the readings with those that would normally be expected. One set of sensors is lowered into the river or lake.
  • Method 2 — insert two sets of sensors, one upstream from the suspected site of pollution and one downstream, immediately after the site (such as a farm or factory). The readings from the two sets are compared to see whether there are differences, so a conclusion can be reached on whether the site is causing pollution.
  • The sensors feed data to an ADC and then the computer processes it.
  • The sensors used are temperature, pH, turbidity, oxygen and CO2 sensors.
  • The processing is a comparison of the readings with normal values (method 1) or a comparison of the readings from the two sets of sensors (method 2).

Environmental monitoring is the collection of data relating to our environment. This could be:

  • Monitoring sound in cities, along with other pollution produced by motor vehicles
  • Monitoring soil quality in outdoor gardens and in greenhouses, using pH, moisture and temperature sensors

Environmental monitoring can also be linked to climate study, and can include:

  • Detecting abnormally high temperatures with temperature sensors, so that people can be warned of the dangers
  • Monitoring air pollution with oxygen and CO2 sensors; pH sensors can also help because they give acidity readings
  • Monitoring ultraviolet levels: excessive UV can cause skin cancer, so governments in high-risk countries often measure it to judge whether people should be advised to wear skin protection cream

Calibration

Calibration is making sure that a sensor gives accurate readings. For example, when a temperature sensor measures boiling water, it should cause the computer to output 100 °C. It is done by comparing the value the sensor produces with a known measurement.

The importance of calibration

  • The accuracy of all sensors reduces over time, because of constant use and exposure to the atmosphere or liquids being measured.
  • Slight erosion of the sensor’s material can also make readings less accurate.
  • Regular calibration helps maintain the accuracy of the sensors.
  • Other devices in a monitoring system, such as the ADC, may deteriorate over time and also need recalibration.

Methods used to calibrate devices

  • To calibrate a temperature sensor you need a known temperature value. This could come from a heat source such as boiling water, which gives an accurate high temperature, or from a mixture of ice and water, which gives an accurate 0 °C.
  • The sensor generates a voltage, which is converted into a temperature reading. The algorithm that does this can be refined so the reading matches the actual temperature.
  • The alternative is to take a reading from a sensor that has already been calibrated and compare the temperature reading of the sensor being calibrated with it.

One-point calibration

  • The easiest type to perform; only one measurement point is needed.
  • This makes it appropriate for sensors that measure a set temperature, or a temperature range, that is constant.
  • It is often needed for sensors constantly used at very high temperatures, which lose accuracy after some time. Doing a one-point calibration every so often and comparing it with the previous calibration shows this.
  • A reading is taken from the sensor in the range being measured and compared with a pre-calibrated sensor or a known value.
  • The sensor reading is subtracted from the known value, which gives an offset.
  • The offset is added to every reading in the temperature range being measured.
Example

A sensor is placed in boiling water (known value 100 °C) and reads 98 °C. The offset is 100 − 98 = +2 °C, so 2 °C is added to every later reading: a reading of 60 °C becomes 62 °C.

Two-point calibration

  • Used with sensors that measure a constantly changing variable.
  • Two readings are taken with the sensor, which lets the relationship between the sensor and the standard (pre-calibrated) sensor be established.
  • It can correct both slope and offset errors.
  • Best used when readings are linear.
  • Readings are taken at the low end and the high end of the range, using a check or reference instrument, or previously known readings.
  • The offset is calculated by comparing with the known readings.

Multi-point calibration

  • Uses more than two readings from the sensor.
  • Readings are taken at a high, a middle and a low known value. For a temperature sensor, the boiling, middle and freezing temperatures of water can be used, i.e. 100 °C, 50 °C and 0 °C.
  • This establishes the relationship between the sensor and the standardised sensor.
  • Best used where readings form a curve, a non-linear relationship.
  • The calibration can’t be done just by adding an offset or allowing for the slope. There is no longer a linear relationship, so the algorithm needs to include curve fitting.
  • It uses a formula to calculate the correction, such as a quadratic: y = ax2 + bx + c, where y is the reading from the standardised sensor and x is the reading from the sensor being calibrated.

Advantages and disadvantages of computer monitoring

This is also the answer to “evaluate the use of computers in monitoring temperature, etc. compared with traditional methods / humans”.

Advantages

  • Humans can’t take readings at very frequent intervals, because they must note each reading, during which they can’t take another. Computers can read at more frequent intervals, and can read more than one variable at the same time, whereas humans can only do one thing at a time and so take longer.
  • It is very difficult for humans to keep taking readings for long periods, whereas computers can be left on at any time, day or night. Readings are always taken at regular intervals, unlike with a human who might forget.
  • It takes people time to draw accurate graphs, whereas computers can produce them automatically after processing the data.
  • Results are produced automatically once the readings are received, unlike a human who would take a lot of time to write them down.
  • Readings taken by computers tend to be more accurate than those taken by humans, because computers are not subject to “human error”.

Disadvantages

  • Computers can be expensive to buy and to maintain, whereas humans would be expected to do the monitoring as part of their job.
  • It is quite difficult to program computers to interpret results, whereas humans can interpret them and are also needed to program the computers in the first place.
  • Sensors can deteriorate after a period of time and need recalibrating, whereas humans tend to be more consistent.
  • If the computer fails, or there is a power cut with no backup supply, readings can be lost.

Control technologies 3.2

Control systems

  • A control system uses microprocessors or computers to control physical variables such as temperature, pressure, humidity, moisture and light.
  • The computer does this by controlling devices that cause the variables to change, or by maintaining certain physical conditions at the same level for a period of time.
  • Control systems use real-time processing.
  • They use actuators to control devices such as motors.
  • The output affects the input.
  • Control systems involve continuous processes.

Example: a temperature-controlled room. The temperature is monitored using sensors. If it falls below a certain value, the microprocessor sends a signal to the heater to switch on, which is the output. The heater being on heats the room and the temperature rises, which immediately affects the new input.

Sensors used in control

SensorDescription and use
Electromagnetic field sensorOften called a magnetic field sensor. It measures the change in the Earth’s natural magnetic field caused by a ferromagnetic object. A vehicle above the sensor distorts the field because of the metal in its body, so its presence is detected. The sensor is small, so it is easier and cheaper to install and maintain than an induction loop, which does more or less the same job. Both are used at car park entrances to control barriers, but magnetic field sensors can also detect the number of spaces available. They are used in some automated car parking systems to help drivers park, in a similar way to ultrasonic sensors.
Ultrasonic sensorMade up of a device that sends out sound waves above the range of human hearing and a sensor that receives the reflected waves. It measures the time taken for the sound to be sent and received; combined with the known speed of sound, the microprocessor can calculate the distance to an object. Used in automated car parking systems to tell the driver how close they are to another vehicle or object so they can park without hitting it.
Proximity sensorUsually a device that sends out a signal (an infrared beam, ultrasound or a magnetic field) and a sensor that receives the reflected signal. One use is in smartphones, to switch off the screen when the phone is held near the ear.
Touch sensorOne type measures fluid levels. A capacitive touch sensor measures the capacitance between two conductors separated by an insulating plate; one conductor is the fluid whose level is being measured. When the fluid touches the sensor, it detects that it is at that level. Often used to measure fluid levels such as the cooling water level in nuclear power plants, to make sure there is enough water to cool the reactors.

Actuators

  • An actuator is a device that turns an electrical signal from a microprocessor into a movement.
  • It is the mechanical part of the control system that provides the output by controlling a device, such as opening a valve.
  • It can also be a motor that controls the opening or shutting of windows in a greenhouse.

Uses of control technology

Greenhouses (glasshouses) — the sensors needed are:

  • A temperature sensor to measure the air temperature in the greenhouse
  • A moisture sensor to measure the water content of the soil
  • A light sensor to measure the light level inside the greenhouse

Maintaining the required temperature

  1. At the start, the user inputs the required temperature (the pre-set value) using a keypad, number pad or touchscreen.
  2. The computer receives temperature data from the temperature sensor.
  3. It is passed to an ADC, which converts the analogue signal to a digital value the computer can understand.
  4. The computer compares the sensor data with the pre-set value.
  5. If the temperature is above the pre-set value, it sends a signal to the window motor to open the window, or to leave it open if it is already open.
  6. If the temperature is below or equal to the pre-set value, it sends a signal to the motor to close the window (or leave it closed), and sends a signal to the actuator to switch the heater on (or leave it on).
  7. The whole process is continuous as long as the system is switched on.
Pseudocode — greenhouse temperature
WHILE system switched on
   INPUT temperature
   IF temperature > pre-set
      THEN
         send signal to window motor to open window or leave it open
         send signal to actuator to switch off heater or leave it off
      ELSE
         send signal to window motor to close window or leave it closed
         send signal to actuator to switch on heater or leave it on
   ENDIF
ENDWHILE

Maintaining the required soil moisture

  1. At the start, the user inputs the required soil moisture level (pre-set value) using a keypad, number pad or touchscreen.
  2. The microprocessor receives data on the amount of moisture in the greenhouse soil from the moisture sensor.
  3. The ADC converts the analogue data into a digital value the computer can understand.
  4. The computer compares the sensor data with the pre-set value input by the user.
  5. If the moisture level is below the pre-set value, a signal is sent to the actuator to open the sprinkler valve, or to leave it open if it is already open.
  6. If the moisture level is above or equal to the pre-set value, a signal is sent to the actuator to close the sprinkler valve, or to leave it closed if it is already closed.
  7. The whole process is continuous as long as the system is switched on.
  • Some greenhouses also have sensors that measure the humidity of the air, because some plants need high humidity as well as warmth.
  • These have separate watering systems: one to spray water into the air and another to put more moisture into the soil.
Pseudocode — greenhouse soil moisture
WHILE system switched on
   INPUT moisture
   IF moisture < pre-set
      THEN
         send signal to actuator to open sprinkler valve or remain open
      ELSE
         send signal to actuator to close sprinkler valve or remain closed
   ENDIF
ENDWHILE

Maintaining the required level of light

  • When the light is not very good, a light source needs to come on so the plants have good growing conditions.
  • A pre-set value still needs to be entered: if the light is less than the pre-set value, the light source is activated.
  • However, when night comes the light source needs to be switched off. A second pre-set value is therefore entered, so that when the amount of light falls below that value, as at night, the light source is switched off.
  • A light sensor measures the amount of light coming into the greenhouse, and analogue to digital conversion takes place.
  • The processing is carried out by the microprocessor.

pre-set1 is the light level below which it is dull during the day; pre-set2 is the light level as it gets dark at night.

Pseudocode — greenhouse lighting
WHILE system switched on
   INPUT light_level
   IF light_level < pre-set1
      THEN
         IF light_level < pre-set2
            THEN
               send signal to actuator to switch off light source or leave off
            ELSE
               send signal to actuator to switch on light source or leave on
         ENDIF
      ELSE
         send signal to actuator to switch off light source or leave off
   ENDIF
ENDWHILE

Central heating systems

  1. The user enters the required temperature using a keypad or touchscreen.
  2. The microprocessor stores the required temperature as a pre-set value.
  3. The microprocessor receives the temperature from the sensor.
  4. The microprocessor compares the temperature from the sensor with the pre-set value.
  5. If the temperature from the sensor is lower than the pre-set value, the microprocessor sends a signal to an actuator to open the gas valves.
  6. If the temperature from the sensor is lower than the pre-set value, the microprocessor sends a signal to an actuator to switch the pump on.
  7. If the temperature is higher than or equal to the pre-set value, the microprocessor sends a signal to switch the pump off and close the valves.
  8. The sequence is repeated until the system is switched off.
Pseudocode — central heating
WHILE system switched on
   INPUT temperature
   IF temperature < pre-set
      THEN
         send signal to open gas valves
         send signal to switch pump on
      ELSE
         send signal to close gas valves
         send signal to switch pump off
   ENDIF
ENDWHILE

Air-conditioning systems

  1. The user enters the required temperature using a keypad or touchscreen.
  2. The microprocessor stores the required temperature as a pre-set value.
  3. The microprocessor receives the temperature from the sensor.
  4. The microprocessor compares the temperature from the sensor with the pre-set value.
  5. If the temperature from the sensor is higher than the pre-set value, the microprocessor sends a signal to an actuator to switch the fans on.
  6. If the temperature is lower than or equal to the pre-set value, the microprocessor sends a signal to switch the fans off.
  7. The sequence is repeated until the system is switched off.

Burglar alarms — microprocessor-controlled burglar alarm systems are used in many houses to protect against intruders. The sensors are:

  • Infra-red sensors to detect the movement of human bodies, which give off heat
  • Sound sensors to detect the level of sound an intruder might make
  • Pressure sensors placed under a carpet or rug, to detect the increase in weight caused by a burglar treading on it

The microprocessor is programmed with certain acceptable levels, and only acts if the sensor readings are greater than these. An ADC is needed so the microprocessor can understand the sensor data. When an intruder is detected, the alarm sounds, a light flashes, and a signal is probably sent to the police to alert them to a possible intruder. The system has to be switched on by the user for this to work.

Control of traffic and pedestrian flow — smart motorways

  • A smart motorway is a section of motorway that uses active traffic management (ATM) techniques.
  • The system uses variable speed limits and/or lets drivers use the hard shoulder at certain times.
  • Computers constantly monitor the road and can change the speed limit or open the hard shoulder to traffic.
  • Normally traffic isn’t allowed on the hard shoulder and a red cross is displayed above it. If traffic becomes congested, the computer opens the hard shoulder by moving the red cross.
  • The computer uses “side-fire radar”, which fires a radar beam at an angle to the roadside.
  • The device measures the speed of the vehicles it is pointed at by detecting the change in frequency of the radar signal when it is reflected back off an oncoming vehicle.
  • All this data is constantly fed back to the computers, which process it and decide what action to take. If the road becomes busy, the system can automatically lower the speed limit or open the hard shoulder to traffic.
  • The computer’s decisions will often be made without human intervention, unless a decision appears to be unusual.

Car park barriers

  • An induction loop is buried below the road surface in front of the barrier.
  • As a car passes over the loop, the metal in the vehicle changes the magnetic field, which causes a change in induction that the loop detects (current flows).
  • The loop sends back data, which is converted to digital. If the computer detects a change compared with a pre-set value, it sends a signal to the actuator, the motor switch, which makes the barrier rise.
  • A light sensor is used to detect when the vehicle has passed beyond the barrier: a light beam passes across the space occupied by the vehicle.
  • If the vehicle stops the light beam reaching the sensor, the microprocessor keeps the barrier raised.
  • When the vehicle is clear, the microprocessor detects that the light beam has resumed and sends a signal to the motor to retract and lower the barrier. This makes sure the barrier stays up until the vehicle has passed through the beam.
  • Ultrasonic sensors are sometimes used in place of light sensors.

Ticket-issuing car park barrier systems

  • Some car park barriers issue a ticket on entry that contains a barcode.
  • When the driver wants to leave, they put the ticket into a machine with a barcode reader and pay for the time parked. The reader feeds the information to the computer, which registers that the barcode belongs to a driver who has paid.
  • As the vehicle leaves, the driver puts the ticket into a machine at the barrier that has another barcode reader. The computer checks whether the barcode matches the list of barcodes belonging to drivers who have paid, and sends a signal to the motor to lift the barrier.
  • Some systems use a magnetic strip instead of a barcode, while others use automatic number plate recognition: the customer types the car’s number plate into the ticket machine before paying. When the car reaches the exit, a camera sends the number plate details to the computer, which uses OCR to read the plate. If the plate matches the paid records, a signal is sent to the motor to lift the barrier.

Traffic lights

  • The computer is programmed to react to different volumes of traffic during the day, and uses induction loops to detect them.
  • The computer receives data from the induction loops by way of an ADC and counts the number of vehicles travelling in each direction.
  • The count is compared with pre-set values, and the computer changes the traffic timings and sequences as required by sending signals to the control box in the traffic lights, which then operates the new sequences or timings.
  • The whole process is continuous.
  • These systems sometimes have to allow for pedestrians crossing at the junction. The pedestrian presses a button at the side of the road; the computer registers this and, after a pre-defined delay, sends a signal to the actuator to change the traffic lights from green to red. It also lights a sign telling pedestrians they can cross.
  • There can be a fair delay if the lights have only just changed, but most modern systems using induction loops can tell whether traffic is light, and so reduce the delay.

Wireless sensor and actuator networks (WSAN)

  • A wireless sensor and actuator network (WSAN) is a group of sensors and actuators that communicate wirelessly.
  • They are called WSANs because actuators can be grouped together to perform a collaborative action, and are then referred to as an actor.
  • An actor works as if it has a microprocessor inside it, because it can make decisions. For example, a robot can be an actor because it contains several actuators in one unit.
  • Examples include smart parking systems, which alert drivers to where car parking spaces are available, and combined heating, ventilation and air conditioning systems.
  • Wireless sensor networks are slightly different: they take no action but simply monitor the environment, such as the wearable devices used to monitor an athlete’s performance.

Smart homes

  • A smart home is a home in which devices and appliances are connected so they can communicate with each other and be controlled by commands from anyone living in the house.
  • A smart home uses the home computer network and router, and is an example of a WSAN.
  • Commands can be given by voice, remote control, tablet or smartphone.
  • The most common devices controlled this way are televisions, music centres, lights, burglar alarms, central heating and air-conditioning units.
  • Smart homes have developed because of the increased use of smartphones and tablets, which are continuously connected to the internet and so can control any online devices.
  • The ability to control devices through a digital network is called the Internet of Things (IoT).
  • Benefit: a homeowner on their way to work can avoid the panic of remembering they forgot to turn the heating off.
  • Risk: smart homes are vulnerable to hackers, who could access the home network and turn off the burglar alarm, or just cause a nuisance such as turning lights off.

Advantages and disadvantages of using control technology

Advantages

  • Microprocessor-controlled burglar alarms give people a greater sense of security, because they feel free from the risk of being burgled
  • Smart homes can reduce the energy needed to heat and light a home, and can save household costs such as electricity by switching appliances off automatically when not needed
  • Computer-controlled traffic lights have led to fewer traffic jams than mechanical or time-controlled ones
  • Air-conditioning in shopping malls makes shopping more comfortable in warm weather
  • Control systems can help people with disabilities who find it difficult to move around to use devices in their home
  • Computer-controlled systems process data more quickly than a human could, so reactions to changes in the inputs are almost immediate
  • People are free to do other things, such as leisure activities, because they don’t have to be in the house to do the cooking or wash clothes or dishes
  • In a computer-controlled greenhouse, a human might forget to take readings or be busy, whereas a computer takes readings at regular intervals, straight away and faster
  • Computers can monitor more than one variable at a time, whereas a human has to take readings one at a time
  • Readings taken by computers are more reliable and accurate, because humans make mistakes
  • Control commands can be scheduled at any time, day or night, without a human being physically present

Disadvantages

  • Computer-controlled car park barriers have increased unemployment: new jobs such as maintenance technicians have been created, but far fewer than the jobs lost
  • Smart homes can make people lazy and over-reliant on microprocessor-controlled devices
  • Smart homes have caused a loss of manual household skills, and stop people doing simple exercise such as walking around or using their hands and arms as much as they used to
  • Air-conditioning can raise costs for shops, which in turn raises prices for consumers
  • Control systems cost a lot in the first place; for example, smart devices are more expensive than non-smart ones
  • A computer-controlled system can’t work if there is a problem with the computer, or a power outage without backup power
